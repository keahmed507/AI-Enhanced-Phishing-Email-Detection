The gap

Content filters miss the attacks that don't look wrong

Phishing emails account for a large share of breaches — and a growing share of them come from accounts that were never technically "spoofed."

65%

of phishing-related attacks are delivered by email — still the cheapest, widest-reaching channel available to attackers.

36%

of organizational data breaches trace back to a phishing email, often one that passed content filters because it came from a legitimate, compromised inbox.

The escalation

Generative AI raised the bar for both sides

The same models that write convincing marketing copy also write convincing phishing emails — which is exactly why detection had to stop relying on content alone.

What attackers gained

AI-generated phishing

  • Generative language models produce personalized, human-sounding phishing content at scale, no longer limited by an attacker's own writing ability.
  • Text is grammatically clean and context-aware, removing the spelling and phrasing errors that keyword and rule-based filters used to catch.
  • Campaigns increasingly pair AI-written text with compromised legitimate accounts, so the message inherits a real sender's trust and history.
  • Persuasion-aware generation techniques deliberately mimic authority, urgency, and rapport — the same psychological levers as human social engineering.
VS
How detection is responding

Context- and behavior-aware defense

  • Research has moved toward transformer-based models (BERT, DistilBERT) that read meaning and context rather than matching surface patterns.
  • A two-stage framework built around persuasion principles reached ~94% accuracy and 98% AUC specifically on AI-generated phishing emails.
  • Hybrid approaches combining BERT with TF-IDF improve accuracy further, especially on smaller, harder-to-generalize datasets.
  • This project's answer is sender behavior profiling: even a perfectly-written AI email still has to come from somewhere — and a sudden shift in a sender's rhythm is much harder for a language model to fake.
Where this project sits today: the content classifier uses TF-IDF, word embeddings, and lexical URL features rather than a transformer. Swapping in a BERT-style model to catch fluent, AI-generated phishing text — alongside the existing behavioral engine — is named directly as future work (Section 6.6).
System design

Four stages, one final decision

A Flask application coordinates the pipeline: every email is parsed, scored two different ways, then fused into a single verdict.

01

Ingest & parse

Splits a raw email into header metadata, body text, and embedded URLs. Malformed messages are rejected before they reach scoring.

02

Extract features

TF-IDF and word embeddings for text, lexical checks for URLs, and per-sender metadata (frequency, timing, tone).

03

Score in parallel

A trained classifier scores content. The profiling engine compares behavior to the sender's historical baseline.

04

Fuse & report

Content label and anomaly score are merged into one decision, logged, and shown on the analyst dashboard.

Module

Feature Extraction Module

Converts raw email content and URLs into a structured feature vector using TF-IDF, embeddings, and lexical URL analysis.

Module

Machine Learning Classifier

Trains and evaluates candidate models — Logistic Regression, Random Forest, SVM, and LSTM — on the content feature vector.

Module

Sender Behavior Profiling Engine

Builds a rolling baseline per sender and scores deviations with Z-score analysis and Isolation Forest.

Evaluation results

Real numbers, on a real dataset

All four classifiers were trained and tested on the Enron-Spam dataset — 33,345 real emails, held out 80/20 with stratified sampling. Spam is used as the phishing-class proxy, the standard substitution in the literature this project cites.

ModelAccuracyPrecisionRecallF1AUC
SVM98.89%98.64%99.17%98.91%99.91%
Logistic Regression98.65%97.95%99.41%98.67%99.85%
Random Forest97.56%95.70%99.64%97.63%99.79%
LSTM91.74%96.38%86.91%91.40%96.11%
URL model (UCI dataset)86.75%92.49%80.00%85.79%92.56%
SVM narrowly leads on this dataset, with Logistic Regression close behind at a fraction of the training cost. The LSTM was trained from scratch for 2 epochs on modest hardware in this environment — it would likely close the gap with more epochs, a larger embedding, or pretrained vectors, but that's a fair reflection of the LSTM's higher data/compute appetite relative to the other three, not a bug. The URL model scores lower because several of the UCI dataset's original features (domain age, web traffic rank, DNS records) require live WHOIS/DNS lookups this offline demo can't perform — it's scored on URL-string features alone. Full methodology, including the sender-behavior evaluation, is in backend/README.md.
